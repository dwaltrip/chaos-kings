# Analysis: Burst-1 Symmetry Breaking

**Date**: 2026-03-21
**Session**: 3.21-2
**Status**: Pre-implementation analysis. Contains theoretical claims that need independent verification before implementation — see "Claims requiring verification" section.

## The idea

On degree-2 boards where the two neighbors of the general have symmetric reachable territories, burst-1 paths through neighbor A and neighbor B produce mirror-image search trees. The solver currently explores both, doing 2x redundant work. If we can detect this symmetry, we skip one neighbor for burst-1 and halve the search.

## Why it matters (and why it might not)

**On toy boards:** The hardest boards in the test suite are corner boards (corner-7x7, corner-9x9, corner-13x13) — all degree-2, all perfectly symmetric. Symmetry breaking would give an instant ~2x on these.

**On real boards:** Real generals.io boards are 25x25+ with random mountain placement. Global symmetry is extremely rare. The question is whether *local* symmetry (symmetric within the reachable radius of the general, even if the rest of the board is asymmetric) occurs often enough to matter. On a 25x25 board, the reachable radius is at most 12 tiles — a small neighborhood. Random mountain placement makes even local symmetry unlikely, but not impossible (e.g., a corner general with no mountains within radius 12).

**Bottom line:** This optimization is high-value on toy corner boards, uncertain value on real boards. It's cheap enough that the downside is just wasted implementation time, not runtime cost.

## Correctness requirement

For symmetry breaking to be safe, we need: *for every solution through neighbor B, there exists an equivalent solution through neighbor A.* If we skip B and miss the only valid solution, the solver breaks.

This means we need **true symmetry of the search tree**, not just a size heuristic. Specifically:
- Every burst-1 path through B must have a reflected path through A with the same length and capturing the same number of new tiles.
- The coveredMask after burst-1 through A must be the reflection of coveredMask after burst-1 through B.
- All subsequent bursts (which also start from the general) must have equivalent candidates in the reflected board region.

In short: the entire reachable territory from both neighbors must be isomorphic under some reflection.

## Detection approaches considered

### 1. Territory size comparison (rejected)

Compare `popcount(blankMasks[maxDist])` between the two neighbors. If equal, assume symmetric.

**Problem:** Equal size ≠ same shape. Two neighbors could have 47 reachable tiles each in completely different configurations. Skipping one could miss the only valid solution. Unsound.

### 2. Board-level geometric check (rejected for generalization)

Check if the entire board is invariant under a reflection that maps neighbor A to neighbor B. For a corner general at (0,0) on a square board: check `board.types[index(r,c)] === board.types[index(c,r)]` for all tiles.

**Pros:** Simple, trivially correct.
**Cons:** Fails on any board with even one mountain outside the reachable radius that breaks global symmetry. On real 25x25 boards, this would almost never fire because distant mountains break the check even when the local neighborhood is perfectly symmetric.

### 3. BFS territory comparison under reflection (proposed)

Compare the actual BFS reachable masks from each neighbor, at each distance, under the appropriate reflection transformation. The data is already precomputed in `NeighborInfo.blankMasks`.

Algorithm:
1. Determine the reflection that maps neighbor A to neighbor B (based on their relative positions to the general).
2. For each distance d from 0 to `maxBurstLen - 1`:
   - Take neighbor A's `blankMasks[d]`
   - Apply the reflection to the bitmask (remap each set bit's position)
   - Compare to neighbor B's `blankMasks[d]`
3. If all distances match → the reachable territories are isomorphic → safe to skip B for burst-1.

**Why this is correct (sketch — see "Claims requiring verification"):**

The key property is that the reflections used (horizontal, vertical, diagonal, anti-diagonal through the general) are **isometries of the grid** — they preserve L1 adjacency. Two tiles that are cardinal neighbors before reflection remain cardinal neighbors after reflection. This is why reflected BFS mask equality implies more than just "same tile counts at each distance":

- Reflected mask equality at every distance means the same tiles (under reflection) are reachable at each BFS depth.
- Because the reflection preserves adjacency, the local connectivity between reachable tiles is also preserved — a reflected edge is still an edge.
- Therefore the subgraph of reachable tiles from neighbor A is isomorphic (under the reflection) to the subgraph from neighbor B.
- This means every valid path through one neighbor has a reflected valid path through the other, covering reflected tiles.
- And since the reflection preserves adjacency at every depth (not just locally), the full search tree — including all subsequent bursts — is isomorphic.

**Why this handles local symmetry:** BFS from each neighbor only explores tiles reachable from that neighbor (excluding the general). Mountains at distance 20 that break global board symmetry don't appear in the BFS masks and don't affect the comparison. Only the local reachable territory matters.

**Why the full reachable territory is covered on degree-2 boards:** On a degree-2 board, the general has exactly 2 non-mountain neighbors. Every tile reachable from the general (excluding the general itself) is reachable through one of these two neighbors. The union of the two neighbor BFS territories IS the full reachable territory. So checking both neighbors' BFS masks under reflection checks the entire search space. The general maps to itself under all the reflections listed below, and is excluded from both BFS masks, so it doesn't affect the comparison.

**Radius:** No need to choose — we check all distances in `blankMasks`, which is already `maxBurstLen - 1` (typically 11). This covers every tile any burst path could reach from either neighbor, which is exactly the territory that matters for search tree equivalence.

## The reflection mapping

For two cardinal neighbors of the general, the reflection axis is determined by their relative positions. The general is always between them (they're adjacent to it). For a general at position `(gr, gc)`:

| Neighbor pair | Reflection | Mapping (relative to general) |
|---|---|---|
| right + down | Diagonal | `(gr+dr, gc+dc) → (gr+dc, gc+dr)` |
| left + up | Diagonal | `(gr+dr, gc+dc) → (gr+dc, gc+dr)` |
| left + down | Anti-diagonal | `(gr+dr, gc+dc) → (gr-dc, gc-dr)` |
| right + up | Anti-diagonal | `(gr+dr, gc+dc) → (gr-dc, gc-dr)` |
| left + right | Vertical axis | `(gr+dr, gc+dc) → (gr+dr, gc-dc)` |
| up + down | Horizontal axis | `(gr+dr, gc+dc) → (gr-dr, gc+dc)` |

Where `(dr, dc)` is the offset from the general. The reflected position might be off-board — if any reflected position is off-board and the original is on-board (or vice versa), symmetry fails, which is correct.

Note: mountain positions don't need to be checked separately. The BFS masks only contain reachable non-mountain tiles. If a mountain at position X has a non-mountain at its reflected position X', the BFS masks will differ (one includes the reflected position, the other doesn't), and the symmetry check correctly fails. Unreachable tiles (mountains or blanks behind mountains) don't appear in either mask and don't affect the comparison.

## Reflecting a bitmask

To apply a reflection to a bitmask, we need to remap each set bit's board position:

```
for each set bit at position p:
  (r, c) = (p / width, p % width)
  (r', c') = reflect(r, c)  // using the appropriate mapping above
  if (r', c') is off-board → symmetry fails
  set bit at (r' * width + c') in the reflected mask
```

This is O(board_size) per mask in the worst case, O(popcount) if we iterate set bits. We do this once per neighbor pair per solve — negligible cost.

## Partial symmetry breaking

Full symmetry within the entire BFS radius (distance 11) is rare on real boards. But **partial** local symmetry — symmetric up to some shorter distance k — is more plausible. For example, a corner general might have no mountains within radius 4, giving local symmetry up to distance 4 even on a cluttered board.

If BFS masks match under reflection at distances 0 through k but not k+1, then burst-1 paths of length ≤ k+1 through neighbor A have mirrors through neighbor B (the path starts at the neighbor and extends k steps, all within the symmetric region).

**However, partial symmetry is harder to exploit safely.** Even if burst-1 itself is within the symmetric radius, subsequent bursts (2+) can reach tiles beyond that radius. The coveredMask after a burst-1 of length 5 is symmetric, but if burst-2 of length 10 reaches into the asymmetric region, the reflected solution might not exist.

For partial symmetry breaking to be correct, we'd need: all tiles reachable by *any* burst (not just burst-1) to be within the symmetric radius. Since all bursts start from the general and can be up to `maxBurst` tiles long, this effectively requires full-radius symmetry again.

An alternative: only exploit partial symmetry when the *total* solution (all bursts combined) stays within the symmetric radius. But we don't know this until we've found the solution, which defeats the purpose.

**Conclusion:** Partial symmetry breaking is tempting but faces the same full-radius requirement once subsequent bursts are considered. It might be possible to make it work with tighter analysis (e.g., if later bursts are constrained to use overlap and therefore stay near the general), but this needs careful verification before implementation. Flagged as a potential follow-up, not part of the initial implementation.

## Potential for slowdown on near-symmetric boards

On a truly symmetric board, solutions through both neighbors appear at the same search depth by symmetry — skipping one neighbor can't slow things down.

However, the BFS mask comparison could theoretically detect symmetry on a board that is symmetric in tile layout but has subtle asymmetries not captured by the check (see "Claims requiring verification"). On such a near-symmetric board, the first solution through the kept neighbor might appear later in the candidate ordering than the first solution through the skipped neighbor. This would make symmetry breaking paradoxically *slower* than the baseline.

In practice, this risk is mitigated by the strictness of the BFS mask comparison (exact reflected equality at every distance). If the check passes, the boards should be truly symmetric. But this should be validated empirically: compare solve times with and without symmetry breaking on all test boards, and confirm no regressions.

## Degree-3+ generalization

For degree-3 or degree-4 generals with some symmetric neighbor pairs, we could skip one neighbor from each symmetric pair. This is more complex:
- Need pairwise comparisons (up to 6 pairs for degree-4)
- Each pair might have a different reflection axis
- Need to ensure we don't skip too many — must keep at least one representative from each equivalence class

**Correctness warning:** On a degree-3+ board, checking that neighbors A and B have symmetric BFS territories is **not sufficient** to skip B. The reason: subsequent bursts can go through neighbor C (the third neighbor), which is not constrained by the A↔B reflection. A solution that uses burst-1 through B and burst-2 through C might not have a valid mirror through A, because C's territory is not symmetric under the A↔B reflection.

For degree-3+ symmetry breaking to be correct, the *entire reachable territory from the general* (through all neighbors) must be symmetric under the reflection, not just the pair's territory. This is a much stronger requirement and would rarely be satisfied on real boards.

This is lower priority — high-degree generals are typically in open positions where the solver is already fast. Would revisit only if profiling showed degree-3+ boards as slow.

## Path generation scalability

On real 25x25 boards, path generation at length 12 could produce dramatically more paths than on toy boards (the architecture reference notes ~178K paths at length 12 on open-11x11). If path generation dominates solve time at realistic scale, all search-phase optimizations (symmetry breaking, group ordering, feasibility pruning) become irrelevant.

This is unknown — phase timing (search vs path-gen breakdown) has never been measured. Adding realistic test boards would answer this question and should come before further search optimization work.

## Interaction with other optimizations

**Group ordering:** Independent and composable. Symmetry breaking reduces burst-1 candidates, group ordering changes which timing entries are tried first. No interaction.

**Feasibility pruning:** No interaction. After burst-1, the board state (coveredMask) determines feasibility checks. With symmetry breaking, we reach the same states (reflected) so feasibility pruning behaves identically.

**L1 neighbor partitioning:** Independent. L1 partitions candidates by starting neighbor for bursts 2+. Symmetry breaking only affects burst-1 neighbor selection.

## Claims requiring verification

The following theoretical claims are central to the approach but involve non-trivial reasoning. They should be independently verified before implementation:

1. **Grid reflections preserve adjacency.** Claim: the 4 reflection types (horizontal, vertical, diagonal, anti-diagonal through a point) map cardinal-adjacent tiles to cardinal-adjacent tiles on a rectangular grid. This is the foundation of the approach — if reflections don't preserve adjacency, reflected BFS masks matching doesn't imply isomorphic path structure.

2. **BFS mask equality under a grid isometry implies subgraph isomorphism.** Claim: if a reflection is an adjacency-preserving map, and the BFS cumulative masks from two starting tiles match under that reflection at every distance, then the induced subgraphs of reachable tiles are isomorphic. The gap to verify: BFS cumulative masks are sets of reachable tiles at each distance — does this fully characterize the subgraph, or could two non-isomorphic subgraphs produce the same BFS layer structure?

3. **Subgraph isomorphism implies search tree isomorphism.** Claim: if the reachable subgraphs from two neighbors are isomorphic, then the set of valid paths (non-backtracking chains) through each is isomorphic, and therefore the solver's search tree is isomorphic. This requires that the path validity rules (non-backtracking, contiguous, no mountains) are preserved under the isomorphism.

4. **Degree-2 coverage.** Claim: on a degree-2 board, the union of BFS territories from both neighbors equals the full reachable territory from the general (minus the general itself). This ensures that checking both neighbors' BFS masks covers the entire search space, including tiles visited by bursts 2+.

5. **Reflection mapping table correctness.** The 6 mappings in the table should each be verified to (a) map the general to itself, (b) swap the two listed neighbors, and (c) be an involution (applying twice gives the identity).

## Open questions

### Does degree-2 detection even help on real boards?

On a real 25x25 board, a general in the interior has 4 neighbors. A general on an edge has 3. A general in a corner has 2. Corner placement is 4 out of 625 possible positions — rare. And even corner generals on real boards likely have mountains within the reachable radius that break local symmetry.

The optimization fires when: (a) general has exactly 2 non-mountain neighbors, AND (b) the reachable territories from those neighbors are symmetric. On real boards, condition (a) can also occur when mountains block 2 of 4 neighbors, creating a "virtual degree-2" even in the interior. But condition (b) is still unlikely with random mountains.

**Possible experiment:** Run the symmetry detection on a sample of real boards to measure how often it fires. If it's <5% of boards, the optimization has limited practical value.

### Priority relative to realistic test boards

This optimization was prioritized when the hard boards were all symmetric corners. Now that we know real boards are 25x25+ with random mountains, adding realistic test boards should probably come first — it would tell us whether symmetry breaking (or any current optimization) matters at all at realistic scale.

## Recommendation

Implement the BFS territory comparison approach (option 3), scoped to degree-2 generals. It's correct (pending verification of the claims above), handles local symmetry, uses precomputed data, and costs ~30-40 lines. But **add realistic test boards first** to validate that the optimization fires on boards that actually matter, before spending time on edge cases and generalization.

Before implementation, the claims in "Claims requiring verification" should be independently checked — either by formal argument or by constructing test cases that would expose a violation.
