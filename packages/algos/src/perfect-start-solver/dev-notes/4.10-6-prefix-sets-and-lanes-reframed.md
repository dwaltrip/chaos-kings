# Prefix Sets and Lanes, Reframed

A conceptual reframing of how we think about the next-gen solver, developed during session 3 (before any implementation on the direction it points at). This doc is about the *why*: how the perfect-start problem decomposes, what the decomposition implies, and what we already know about its shape. Specific mechanisms, data structures, and results live in the session notes.

**Terminology.** Shorthand the rest of the doc leans on:

- **Burst** — a series of moves that captures N new tiles in sequential ticks. May traverse already-captured tiles (re-traversals / overlaps), so total move cost = N captures + M re-traversals. Usually referred to by N (e.g. "a burst of 5").
- **Prefix** — the opening moves of a burst, navigating off the general's local environment into position for main expansion.
- **Tip** — the last tile of a prefix; where main expansion (the lane) begins.
- **Lane** — the main-expansion path of a burst — long, length-dominated. Concept goes back to the corridor / lane model in [session 4](../explore-landscape/docs/sessions/session-4-summary.md).
- **Profile** — the sequence of burst sizes composing the opening round, e.g. `[5,3]` = two bursts capturing 5 and 3 new tiles respectively. The eventual solver's output is both the profile and the concrete paths that realize it.
- **Face projection / greedy longest-path** — the two cheap lane-extension heuristics evaluated in [session 2](4.10-4-lane-decomp-session-2-summary.md).

## The two-problem decomposition

The full "perfect start" problem is hard. We don't have a clean attack on it as a single monolithic search, and the heuristic progress we've made so far (sessions 1–2) has been on individual aspects rather than the whole.

A productive split: think of the problem as **two mostly-independent sub-problems** with a small interface between them.

**Problem 1 — opening / prefix.** How do we get off the general into regions from which expansion is possible? This is near-region work — small search space, topology-dominated. The general sits in a specific local environment (corner, corridor, pocket, open space), and the first few moves of each burst have to navigate that local environment before anything else matters.

**Problem 2 — main expansion / lanes.** Given that we've landed at some good starting positions, how do we construct the long expansion paths that make up the bulk of a round? This is mid-region work — larger search space, length-dominated rather than topology-dominated. Sessions 1–2 explored it under the name "lane decomposition."

These sub-problems have different characters. Problem 1 has a small search space but is topologically tricky: one wrong step off the general and you're in a dead-end you can't recover from. Problem 2 has a larger search space but simpler local structure: once you're in open enough space for lanes to exist at all, the hardness is about combinations rather than navigation.

**Why split rather than solve jointly?** Because the two have different cost structures and different sources of hardness. A heuristic trying to address both at once has to reason about local topology *and* combinatorial path length *and* overlap constraints simultaneously. Splitting lets each sub-problem use the heuristics that match its shape.

**The interface between them:** one shared object — **prefix tips are lane starting positions**. On boards where the decomposition is clean, that's the whole coupling, and that's what makes the decomposition productive. Real boards will surface additional couplings (see Known tensions) — we treat those as refinements to a mostly-clean interface, not a rejection of it.

## "Good prefix = good lane entry point"

The decomposition raises an immediate question: when is a prefix "good"?

We could invent various notions — prefix captures the most tiles, prefix spreads across the most directions, prefix has the nicest shape — but most of those are indirect proxies for something the decomposition names directly:

> **The dominant factor in prefix quality is whether the tip is a position from which a lane of the required length can be constructed.**

The reframe's central bet is that tip quality is the dominant term in prefix quality — that most of what distinguishes a good prefix from a bad one is whether its tip can support a lane. Other factors exist (the Known tensions section catalogs the ones we already suspect: interior cost, joint quality across multiple tips, extreme geometries), and real boards will surface more. We expect them to be corrections on top of the tip-quality term, not replacements for it.

**The scoring work is shared.** Session 2 built face projection as a "can a lane of length L extend from this tile?" filter — a lane-decomposition heuristic. Under the reframe, that same function also measures tip quality. Session 2's heuristics and whatever we build next aren't "lane-decomposition progress" on one side and "prefix-generation progress" on the other; they're a single shared scoring layer that does the heavy lifting for both sub-problems. That's the unification.

Every cheap lane-entry signal is automatically a cheap prefix-quality signal. Every investment in refining the scoring layer pays off on both sides at once. And the unification holds whatever the exact size of the tip-quality term — the per-tile scoring work is needed either way.

One caveat worth naming: the unification cuts both ways, so whatever precision/recall the lane filter has, the prefix scorer inherits. Face projection's 76% recall on the session 2 matrix implies a face-projection-based prefix scorer would reject roughly a quarter of viable tips in turning-lane regimes. Refining the scoring layer is the load-bearing work.

## What this suggests about structure

The reframe has natural structural implications, but we want to be careful not to over-commit. The specific shape of the eventual solver will emerge during implementation and will probably look different from any sketch we draw today.

That said: the framing inverts the usual pattern of "smart generator, simple scoring." If scoring is cheap enough, the generator doesn't need to be clever — it can propose many candidates and let the scorer filter them. The more the scoring layer develops, the more the center of gravity shifts from generator complexity toward scorer sophistication.

The important conceptual point is not the specific pipeline. It's that **one body of work serves both sub-problems**, and whatever we build on the scoring side cashes in for both.

## What changes about our mental model

Before the reframe, we were implicitly treating prefix generation and lane decomposition as independent problems. Session 1 built an oracle for lane decomposition. Session 2 built heuristic filters for it. Both sessions touched on prefix generation only indirectly — as "blob generators" — and the results became increasingly suspect as session 2 progressed (see the blob-pathology finding in [session 2 findings §5](4.10-3-lane-decomp-session-2-findings.md)). The two research threads were running in parallel without clean coupling.

Under the reframe, they collapse into a single thread. The heuristic progress already made — face projection, greedy longest-path, and whatever we add later — isn't "progress on half the solver." It's progress on the shared scoring layer that both halves need.

This also recasts session 2's empirical findings as consequences of a shared underlying question: **is there an objective function that measures tip quality?** Until that question is answered, blob generators drift without a compass, face projection is measuring a proxy for something nobody has named, and "hard" boards are ones where the shared scoring layer doesn't yet have an answer. Once the question is answered — even partially — many of those findings start to look less like independent problems and more like symptoms of the same gap.

If prefixes don't deliver usable lane entry points, the prefix stage isn't buying anything the downstream lane construction couldn't do on its own — at which point there's no reason to have a prefix stage at all, and we'd be better off going back to the full-length path enumeration / pruning approach of the current solver. Blob pathology is the sharpest evidence that this matters. It's exactly what "prefixes that fail to deliver usable tips" looks like in practice: without a scoring signal to steer toward, the generator has no way to distinguish a useful blob from a stranded one, and the result is prefix sets that strand the frontier where lanes can't extend. So "good prefix = good tip" isn't a stylistic preference; it's the only condition we've identified that justifies having a separate prefix stage at all. Other justifications could exist in principle — some structural benefit of prefix paths we haven't noticed — but we don't know of anything concrete, and the lane-entry-quality payoff seems strong enough to carry the reframe on its own.

## Known tensions

A few things we're aware of but haven't resolved. These are high-level tensions to keep in mind as the framing gets implemented, not specific technical questions we need to answer today.

- **The interface may not be perfectly clean.** The reframe's central bet is that tip quality is the *dominant* term in prefix quality, not the only one. Prefix interior cost, overall turn budget, and burst profiles all couple back into what counts as "good enough," and real boards will almost certainly surface cases where the tip-quality term alone isn't sufficient. How big the residual corrections are — and whether they can stay "corrections on top" or need to restructure the scoring layer itself — is an open question.

- **Per-tip scoring is local; actual quality is joint.** Two tips that each score well independently may not remain good as a set — their best paths might collide, or one might consume tiles another needs. How much this bites in practice is unknown.

- **Extreme geometries may force overlap.** On highly constrained boards (degree-2 near the general, single-exit pockets), the two sub-problems may not cleanly separate. Multiple bursts may have to share near-region neighbors, blurring the line between "opening" and "main expansion."

None of these are reasons to abandon the framing. They're reasons to expect it to need refinement as it meets the structural diversity of real boards.

## Validation so far

Session 3 built a cheap topological analysis scoped to the region near the general. Most of that work is mechanism rather than framing, but one concrete data point is worth noting:

**The `tight-corner-1` chute — the one case [session 2](4.10-3-lane-decomp-session-2-findings.md) identified as genuinely structurally hard — is identifiable purely from topology.** Brute-force lane decomposition isn't needed to find the chute. The chute tiles are visible as articulation points and bridges from Tarjan analysis on the starting region (after filtering boundary artifacts — see [session 3 notes §4](4.10-7-session-3-notes.md)).

That's a single data point, not a proof of the framing. But it shows that even a tier-1 structural signal catches the case session 2 found hardest empirically, without running any of the expensive lane-decomposition work. If the shared scoring layer can recognize the chute from topology, then a prefix-side consumer of that layer can avoid session 2's failure mode on `tight-corner-1` without having to touch the chute problem algorithmically.

It's the first concrete sign the reframe has teeth. Details of the implementation, the per-board annotations, and the spot checks live in the session 3 note.

## Side note: the "starting region" concept

Session 3 introduced a piece of machinery worth naming: the **starting region**, defined as the set of tiles reachable from the general within a capped BFS radius. Starting regions are small (~50–150 tiles on typical boards), so graph analyses that would be prohibitive on the full board — distance transforms, Tarjan, local width measurement — run essentially for free in absolute terms.

This is a scoping trick that makes per-tile analysis actionable. It's mechanism, not framing; the reframe above doesn't depend on this specific scoping choice, only on *some* way of making per-tile analysis cheap near the general. The session 3 note covers the implementation and what's in it.

## Prior work and conceptual lineage

The lane-decomposition framing that sessions 1–2 explored didn't appear from nowhere. It emerged from the **corridor / N-corridor models** developed in the `explore-landscape` work — see [session 4 summary](../explore-landscape/docs/sessions/session-4-summary.md) for the fullest version.

Those sessions looked at radically simplified boards — an infinitely long corridor with the general at one end, then N parallel corridors — and found clean **state-collapse and equivalence properties** that held across the generalization. The lane model emerged as a natural bridge from these simplified corridors toward open 2D boards: wider corridors decompose into parallel lanes with per-burst traversal overhead proportional to distance from the general's column.

Whether the state-collapse findings carry over to the full solver is an open question. They depend on having a compact abstract state that holds on real boards, which we haven't established. But the earlier results are worth noting for two reasons:

1. They are the **conceptual ancestor** of the lane-decomposition idea that this doc is now unifying with prefix generation. The decomposition we're using is not arbitrary — it was suggested by the shape of the corridor results, and the reframing here builds on that foundation.
2. If a compact abstract state ever becomes tractable on real boards, the corridor findings are already there as theoretical grounding. The structural properties of state collapse, frontier bounds, and per-burst overhead are worth considering as we move toward an actual solver — even if we don't use them directly for the scoring-layer work the reframe points at.

For now, the reframe in this doc is about *how to think about the decomposition*, not about any specific state representation. But the corridor work is a good reference for what kinds of algebraic simplifications this domain admits at the edges.
