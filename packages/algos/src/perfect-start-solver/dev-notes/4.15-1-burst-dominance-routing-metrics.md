# Research spike 2: Burst dominance & routing metrics

The solver's fundamental bottleneck is **search space size** — especially the cost of proving infeasible capture targets impossible before descending. Reducing the number of burst candidates to search is high leverage. This session explores whether cheap routing metrics can formalize the intuition that some burst-1 candidates are strictly better than others — enabling dominated candidates to be skipped entirely.

## The example bursts

6×6 board with the general on the right edge, 2 mountains creating a small pocket above-right. General has degree 2 (left neighbor, down neighbor). See `scratchpad/6x6-pocket-burst-dominance-2.txt` for the 7-tile versions.

```
· · · · · ·
· · · · · ·
· · · · # #
· · · · · G
· · · · · ·
· · · · · ·
```

Six candidate burst-1 paths (all 7 tiles), organized into three groups:

- **Group B (best):** Goes left from the general, then curves north around the mountains into the pocket. Captures the hard-to-reach tiles above the mountains — tiles that ANY burst reaching them must take the same expensive detour for. Stays along the periphery, hugging the obstacle boundary. Leaves the entire open south/west region connected and accessible.

- **Group A (middle):** Goes left from the general into the open territory. A-2 goes straight west, creating a wall across the middle of the board. A-1 zigzags southwest. Both leave the mountain pocket uncaptured — some future burst must still pay the detour cost. A-2's wall partitions the remaining territory into north and south sub-regions, forcing future bursts to commit to one side. Still, A pushes further from the general than C does — its tiles are more peripheral, leaving near-general routing options less damaged. Future bursts can still depart the general and reach open territory without as much overlap.

- **Group C (worst):** Stays compact near the general. C-1 fills a dense block immediately left and below the general, consuming prime near-general real estate. C-2 spirals through BOTH neighbors of the general, walling it off almost entirely — future bursts must overlap just to leave. The tiles C captures are close to the general and easily reachable by other bursts — there's no reason to "waste" burst-1 on them when later, shorter bursts could grab them cheaply. Maximum local routing damage for minimum strategic value.

The intuition: **B handles the constrained, expensive-to-reach tiles and leaves open territory free. A cuts through open territory, creating routing obstacles. C consumes the most contested near-general space.** Anything achievable with A as burst-1 should be achievable with B, and possibly more. Same for A vs C.

The rest of this session was brainstorming ways of making this intuition mathematically rigorous — finding cheap, computable metrics that capture "routing freedom in the residual" and could potentially prove dominance relationships between burst candidates.

## The dominance idea

If burst X dominates burst Y, then after fully searching X as burst-1, Y can be skipped entirely — anything achievable with Y is also achievable with X. On a board where dominance eliminates 80-90% of burst-1 candidates, the inner loop shrinks by an order of magnitude.

The key insight: dominance isn't about which tiles are captured — it's about **optionality in the residual**. And the routing damage isn't binary (partitioned vs not). It's a **continuum of routing tax** — even a partial wall that doesn't fully partition the board still costs overlap ticks to cross, reducing what future bursts can achieve.

## Metrics

Two complementary measures, both cheap to compute:

- **Routing edges** (sum of fresh-neighbor counts within distance D) = 2 × fresh-to-fresh edges in the residual. Measures internal connectivity — how many routing options exist between uncaptured tiles. A wall destroys edges on both sides; a peripheral burst only on one.
- **Fresh-only reachability** (BFS from the general through uncaptured tiles only) = tiles accessible at 0 overlap cost. Measures accessibility from the general — a residual can be well-connected internally but walled off from the general (C-2's failure case).

**Distance-bounded cumulative analysis** is key — computing these metrics within distance D from the general, for varying D. The near-general region (D≤4 to D≤6) is where bursts separate most clearly. Global aggregates wash out the differences.

## Experimental results

### Routing edges (delta from baseline, within distance D)

Higher = less routing damage = better.

| Burst | D≤3 | D≤4 | D≤5 | D≤6 |
|-------|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| B-1   | -10 | **-14** | -20 | -24 |
| B-2   | -10 | **-14** | -20 | -26 |
| A-2   | -10 | -16 | -22 | -28 |
| A-1   | -14 | -26 | -32 | -34 |
| C-2   | -20 | -28 | -28 | -28 |
| C-1   | -18 | -30 | -34 | -34 |

At D≤4, the B group (-14) is clearly separated from A (-16 to -26) and C (-28 to -30).

### Fresh-only reachability (tiles reachable from the general without crossing captured territory)

| Burst | D≤4 | D≤5 | D≤6 | D≤8 |
|-------|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| B-1   | 10  | 15  | 22  | **26** |
| B-2   | 10  | 15  | 19  | **22** |
| A-2   | 7   | 9   | 11  | **12** |
| A-1   | 4   | 4   | 4   | **4** |
| C-1   | 2   | 2   | 2   | **2** |
| C-2   | 0   | 0   | 0   | **0** |

B has access to 22-26 of 26 fresh tiles at zero overlap. C-2 has access to literally zero (both neighbors captured). The separation is stark.

### Key observations

- Neither metric alone is sufficient — routing edges miss the accessibility problem (C-2 ties B-1 on edges despite being walled off), fresh-only-reach misses internal connectivity. Together they cleanly separate B >> A >> C.
- Longer bursts (7 vs 6 tiles) amplify the signal — paths "commit" more to their shape, compounding routing consequences.

## Timing model (max_tick=30)

- Burst-1 (7 captures, 0 overlap): starts tick 15, ends tick 21
- Max achievable: **14 total captures** (7 + 7 remaining). 16 is impossible.
- 22 timing entries with burst-1=7 at 14 captures. Manageable for exhaustive search.
- Simplest remaining patterns: `[7,4,2,1]`, `[7,3,3,1]`, `[7,3,2,2]` (zero overlap)

## Where things stand

The routing metrics are a promising first stab. They correlate well with the intuitive dominance ordering on this toy board — B >> A >> C shows up clearly in both metrics, especially at moderate distance cutoffs. The fact that two independently motivated measures (connectivity and accessibility) agree on the same ordering is encouraging.

But we haven't formally established dominance. The metrics show that B's residual "looks better" than A's by these measures, but we haven't proven that a higher routing score guarantees at least as many achievable captures. It's possible that a burst with worse routing scores could still outperform in some edge case we haven't considered. The gap between "these numbers correlate with burst quality" and "these numbers prove burst X can be skipped" is the hard part.

The next step toward rigor is ground-truth validation: actually solve the remaining sub-problem after each burst-1 candidate and see if the metrics predict the right winner. If they do consistently — across this board and others — that builds confidence even without a formal proof. And the timing model (max_tick=30, 22 feasible entries at 14 captures) makes this tractable on the toy board.

## Open threads

- **Ground truth validation:** Run the actual solver with each burst-1 candidate, compare total captures. Do the routing metrics predict the right winner?
- **Min-overlap Dijkstra:** For each fresh tile, compute minimum captured tiles on any path from general. Directly connects routing cost to tick/overlap budgets.
- **Articulation points** in the fresh subgraph as a fragmentation score.
- **Rigorous dominance criterion:** Can we prove "searched B, safe to skip A and C"? Needs a formal argument that B's residual supports at least as many valid completions.
- **Test on real boards** where the solver is actually slow (corners, pockets, edges).
- **Other cheap metrics** worth exploring: effective general degree, surface-to-volume ratio of captured region, overlap-k reachability (generalize fresh-only-reach to k=1,2,3).
