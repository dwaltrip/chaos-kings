# Research: search space reduction for the perfect-start solver

---

## Problem statement

The **perfect-start problem**: find a move sequence for round 1 (50 ticks) that maximizes tiles captured on a generals.io-style board.

The standard opening strategy is a **burst pattern** — accumulate troops on the general, then deploy them to capture a chain of tiles. This repeats 3-6 times across 50 ticks. Key mechanics:

- General produces +1 troop every 2 ticks (first production on tick 2)
- Moving from tile A to adjacent tile B sends all-but-one troops
- Later bursts may re-traverse owned tiles (**overlap**) to reach fresh territory — costs ticks but not troops
- Feasible burst patterns (sizes, timing, overlap) can be pre-computed from the production schedule

The solver must choose both the burst pattern and the path for each burst, given board geometry (mountains, edges). The naive search space is enormous — ~300 non-backtracking paths at length 6, so 4 bursts of length 6 alone gives ~8 billion combinations. The core challenge is reducing this space efficiently.

---

## What we've learned from two solver attempts

### Algo-1: strong pruning makes brute-force work

Algo-1 pre-generates ALL non-backtracking paths from the general, pre-generates all valid timing entries, then searches for compatible pairs using grouped backtracking with multi-level pruning.

**Why it's fast on most boards:** The timing entry ordering (longest burst first, zero-overlap first) means the simplest valid solution is tried first. On most boards, the FIRST entry produces a solution — hence sub-100ms solves. Feasibility pruning (per-burst reach, aggregate reach, neighbor bottleneck) kills dead branches before candidate scanning. Grouped search amortizes candidate checks across entries with matching `(moveLen, overlap)`. Bitmask operations make all of this cache-friendly.

**Where it hits a wall:** Two sources of search cost on hard boards:

1. **Descent cost:** When max captures < 24, the solver must prove each higher target infeasible before descending. The bulk of runtime is spent exhaustively searching at impossible targets.

2. **Within-target grinding:** Even at the *correct* capture target, certain boards require millions of candidate checks. Corner-9x9 is a completely open 9x9 grid with the general in the corner — 24 captures is achievable, but it takes 3.8s and 20M candidate checks. Pocket-2-11x11 is a wide-open 11x11 with a small 3-mountain pocket near the general — only takes 3 moves to exit, but still 534ms and 2M candidate checks. These boards aren't "constrained" in overall topology — the issue is local geometry near the general (corners, pockets, edges) creating overlap contention between bursts, producing huge numbers of plausible-but-failing combinations.

### Algo-2: construction without pruning is a dead end

Algo-2 tried to exploit a structural decomposition: split the board into a near-region (handled by prefix path enumeration) and a mid-region (handled by greedy lane construction). But the prototype failed badly — orders of magnitude slower, often finding fewer captures. Construction without effective pruning collapses to generate-and-test.

**The lane decomposition idea** — that the mid-region can be partitioned into independent non-overlapping lanes, each owned by a burst — remains a potentially useful structural concept, even though algo-2's specific implementation didn't work.

---

## The core diagnosis

**Search space reduction is the fundamental challenge.** Pruning, state-collapse, or some other way of cheaply eliminating large swaths of the search space is non-negotiable for a fast optimal solver. Both the descent problem and within-target grinding are manifestations of the same issue: the search space is too large to scan without stronger reduction.

## Your task

This is a deep independent research session. Your goal is to study the problem structure, reason through approaches, and produce a well-reviewed document cataloging concrete strategies for search space reduction.

### Phase 1 — Study the problem

Build a concrete mental model of where the search space is large and why. This is the most important phase — don't rush past it.

- **Read the key docs** listed below (ANALYTICAL-REFERENCE, algo-1 README)
- **Read the actual code**: algo-1's search loop, feasibility checks, bitmask operations. Understand mechanically what happens when the solver grinds — where do the 20M candidate checks on corner-9x9 actually go?
- **Study the slow-search boards**: run the solver with `--profile` on various boards (see the `slowSearch` board list). Examine where candidate checks concentrate, what the feasibility pruning catches vs. misses.
- **Study the timing table structure**: understand what timing entries look like, how many there are per capture target, what makes some targets have more entries than others.
- **Look at actual board files** in `test-boards-data/simple/` — understand the geometry of the boards that cause trouble.

### Phase 2 — Generate candidate strategies

Brainstorm approaches to reducing the search space. Consider multiple angles:

- Pruning (killing dead branches earlier/cheaper)
- State collapse (recognizing equivalent configurations)
- Better ordering (trying likely-successful candidates first)
- Structural decomposition (breaking the problem into independent sub-problems)
- Feasibility bounds (cheaply determining what's achievable on a board)
- Anything else that emerges from your study

Draw on the additional reference docs as needed. Explore broadly before committing depth to any single direction.

For each candidate strategy, reason through:
- What structural property does it exploit?
- How much of the search space could it eliminate?
- What are the risks, limitations, or assumptions?
- What open questions remain?

### Phase 3 — Draft findings

Save a draft document to `dev-notes/4.13-3-search-space-reduction-findings.md` with:
- **Key observations** from the study phase — what did you learn about where/why the solver struggles?
- **Catalog of candidate strategies** — structured consistently per the format above

Commit the draft before moving to Phase 4 — we want git history showing how the findings evolve through review.

The reviewer should NOT have seen your study process — fresh eyes are the point. If a finding can't be evaluated by someone reading the draft and reference docs, it's not well-articulated enough.
