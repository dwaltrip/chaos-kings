## 1. Setup

### What we're measuring

For every cell in a `(board, seed, profile)` matrix, we run three things on the same generated blob:

1. **Oracle** — the session-1 exhaustive enumerator with `maxDecomps=1`. A cheap "does any valid decomposition exist at these target lane lengths?" check.
2. **Face projection** — straight rays cast from each frontier tile in each cardinal direction, stopping at mountain/edge/blob. Feasibility verdict = "can we greedily assign k distinct frontier tiles to the k lane lengths such that each tile has a ray of sufficient depth in some direction?" One-sided filter by construction.
3. **Plain greedy** — sort lane lengths descending, DFS a non-backtracking path of exact length from frontier tiles in index order, first path found wins, mark its tiles as used, move on. No cross-lane backtracking. Binary "found / didn't find" result.

**Oracle caveat.** "Oracle exists" always means *"at these specific target lane lengths, using this specific blob shape, the enumerator finds at least one valid non-overlapping non-backtracking decomposition."* It is **not** an absolute feasibility claim about the board — feasibility is a joint function of (board geometry, blob shape, requested lane lengths), and §5 shows exactly how sensitive it is to the blob.

### Matrix

**7 boards × 4 seeds × 3 profiles = 84 cells.**

| Regime | Boards | Blob config |

## 3. Face projection — precision story and the turning-lanes blind spot

### How it works

For each frontier tile (walkable, non-blob, adjacent to the blob — per the codebase's existing `getFrontier` convention), cast a straight ray in each of the 4 cardinal directions. The ray starts at the frontier tile itself and advances one tile per step until it hits a mountain, the board edge, or a blob tile. Depth = number of tiles in the ray (including the starting frontier tile).

Feasibility check: sort requested lane lengths descending, sort frontier tiles by max-over-directions depth descending, greedily assign longest lane to deepest frontier tile. Return `feasible` iff every lane gets a tile with sufficient max depth.

That's the entirety of the filter. A few hundred lines of code in `face-projection.ts`.

### Confusion matrix (reach-more, 84 cells)

|  | Oracle: exists | Oracle: no |
|---|---|---|
| **Face: feasible** | TP = 58 | FP = 0 |
| **Face: infeasible** | FN = 18 | TN = 8 |

- Precision = 58 / 58 = **100%**
- Recall = 58 / 76 = 76%

### On the precision claim

Face projection is designed to be one-sided — it only reports "feasible" when each requested length has some frontier tile with sufficient straight-line depth, with distinct tiles for distinct lanes. Importantly, it does **not** verify that the rays it implicitly assigns are mutually non-overlapping, so a positive verdict isn't a proof that a specific straight-line decomposition exists — it's a claim that one likely does, which the oracle confirmed on all 58/58 positive verdicts in this matrix (possibly via different paths than face projection was picturing). **Precision is 100% in this matrix; whether it would hold under adversarial construction is not proven here.**

### Recall — the turning-lanes blind spot

Face projection misses 18 of 76 oracle-feasible cells (76% recall). The failure mode is not a bug — it's the fundamental limitation of straight-line reasoning. A ray walks cardinally until obstructed; a lane can turn around obstacles. Any board geometry that forces lanes to turn will defeat face projection.

12 of the 18 false negatives cluster on a single board: `scattered-pockets-13x13`. Removing it, face recall over the remaining 64 oracle-feasible cells is 58/64 = 91%. The blind spot is real but geographically concentrated.

### Canonical example — scattered-pockets-13x13

The board geometry near the general (seed 1, reach-more blob):

```
. . . . . # # # . . . . .
# # . # # # # # # # # . #
. . B B B B S B B B B B .
# . # # # # # # # # . # #
. . . . . # # # . . . . .
```

The general sits at `(6, 6)`, inside a 1-tile-high horizontal corridor. The blob fills the entire corridor (10 tiles). Mountains sit above and below the corridor, with narrow gaps punched through at specific columns.

**Frontier = 5 tiles**, all in the gaps — none of them have significant depth in any *straight* cardinal direction, because walls close in within 2–3 tiles in every direction.

The oracle finds, for `[6, 4, 3]`, a valid decomposition:

```
# # # # . # . # . # # # #
. 1 1 . . # . # . . 2 2 .
1 1 1 . . # # # . . . 2 .
# # 1 # # # # # # # # 2 #
. 3 B B B B S B B B B B .
# 3 # # # # # # # # . # #
. 3 . . . # # # . . . . .
. . . . . # . # . . . . .
```

Lane 1 is `(2,5) → (2,4) → (2,3) → (1,3) → (1,4) → (0,4)` — length 6, direction sequence N, N, W, S, W (**three turns**). The straight-ray depth from `(2,5)` in any cardinal direction is no more than 3. Face projection fires "infeasible" for L6 because no frontier tile has straight depth ≥ 6, even though a turning path of length 6 exists.

Generalizing: **"tube-with-gaps" geometries force every lane to turn by construction**, which is exactly the case face projection cannot express. On corner, edge, and open boards it does well; on tube boards it does not.

### Where face projection works

Per the breakdown table in §2, face recall is:
- **100%** on `corner-13x13` (all profiles), `tight-edge-with-chokes` (short and medium), `sparse-mtns-11x11` (short and medium), and `3.22-fairly-open` (all profiles).
- Imperfect on `sparse-mtns-11x11 long` (1/4), `tight-edge-with-chokes long` (3/4), and `tight-corner-1 short` (2/4).
- Zero on `scattered-pockets-13x13` (all profiles).

Corner and edge boards are the natural home for face projection. Tube boards are the natural failure case. Length-cliff cases (`sparse-mtns long`, `tight-edge long`) are where straight rays start to run out of room even on otherwise-accommodating boards.

### Deferred extensions

- **Short BFS instead of straight rays** — replace each ray with a depth-limited BFS, capturing turns up to some radius. More expensive; might fix the scattered-pockets blind spot.
- **8-region framing** from the brainstorm doc — cardinal + diagonal regions. Aggregates multiple nearby frontier tiles into combined depth estimates. Still straight-line-flavored.
- **Length-aware max-flow** — a fundamentally different filter that could catch cases face projection misses (especially chute collisions at long profiles). Still cheap. See §7.

## 4. Greedy longest-path — recall story and the ordering trap

### How it works

1. Sort lane lengths descending.
2. For each length L in order:
   - Walk frontier tiles in sorted-by-index order.
   - For each frontier tile, run a length-limited non-backtracking DFS with obstacle mask = blob ∪ already-used tiles.
   - First path of exactly L tiles found wins — add its tiles to the used set, move on.
   - If no frontier tile yields a path of length L, decomposition fails.

No cross-lane backtracking. No randomization. The DFS neighbor order is deterministic (up → down → left → right), inherited from sibling modules. This is the most literal reading of "plain greedy longest path" — and it lets us measure the pure cost of the greedy ordering assumption.

### Confusion matrix (reach-more, 84 cells)

|  | Oracle: exists | Oracle: no |
|---|---|---|
| **Greedy: found** | TP = 67 | FP = 0 |
| **Greedy: failed** | FN = 9 | TN = 8 |

- Recall = 67 / 76 = **88%**
- Precision is trivially 100% — greedy only reports "found" when it constructs an actual valid decomposition, so FP is structurally zero.

Recall is the interesting number. In this matrix, plain greedy finds a valid decomposition in 88% of oracle-feasible cells. The remaining 9 misses concentrate on one board.

### Failure mode — DFS ordering lock-in on `pocket-11x11`

8 of the 9 greedy misses live on `pocket-11x11`. The trace below walks through the simplest case — seed 1 under *baseline* blob params, which fails greedy at profile `[5, 3]`. Under reach-more, seed 1 actually succeeds (its blob grows larger and gains more frontier tiles), while seeds 2–4 end up with frontier=3 and show the same ordering trap that the baseline seed 1 trace illustrates. The trace is a compact version of the failure mode; the reach-more failures have the same shape with different specifics.

Board + blob near the general:

```
. . . . .
. # # # #
. . . B S
. . B B B
. . . . B
```

General at `(10, 8)` (the `S`), blob = `{(10,8), (9,8), (8,9), (9,9), (10,9), (10,10)}` (6 tiles). Mountains form a ceiling at `y=7` above the blob.

Frontier = 4 walkable non-blob tiles adjacent to the blob: `{(8,8), (7,9), (8,10), (9,10)}` at indices `{96, 106, 118, 119}`. For profile `[5, 3]`:

1. Greedy sorts lengths to `[5, 3]`. Tries L5 first.
2. First frontier tile by index is `(8,8)`. DFS starts there.
3. DFS order U, D, L, R: from `(8,8)`, U is mountain, D is blob, L is `(7,8)` — recurse.
4. From `(7,8)`: U is mountain, D is `(7,9)` — recurse.
5. From `(7,9)`: U visited, D is `(7,10)` — recurse.
6. From `(7,10)`: U visited, D is off-board, L is `(6,10)` — recurse.
7. `(6,10)` is depth 5. Length-5 lane committed: `[(8,8), (7,8), (7,9), (7,10), (6,10)]`.

Now greedy tries L3. Covered tiles include the 5 just claimed.

- `(8,10)` remains as frontier. DFS: U blob, D off-board, L `(7,10)` used, R `(9,10)` — recurse to `(9,10)`. From `(9,10)`: U blob, D off-board, L `(8,10)` visited, R `(10,10)` blob. Dead end at depth 2.
- `(9,10)` remains as frontier. DFS: symmetric dead end.

**Greedy fails to find an L3 path, despite at least one valid decomposition existing.** A separate `run-lane-decomp.ts` inspection on this cell (not the matrix run, which caps at `maxDecomps=1`) showed multiple valid decompositions. The first one the enumerator returns uses an L5 lane `[(8,8), (7,8), (7,9), (6,9), (6,8)]` — same starting tile and same first three moves, but turning *left* at `(7,9)` instead of *down*. That alternative leaves `(7,10)` and `(6,10)` free, making the L3 lane `[(8,10), (7,10), (6,10)]` trivially available.

In other words: greedy commits to the first L5 path its DFS finds, and in a constrained pocket the first path frequently eats the tiles an L3 lane needs. A single different DFS choice at `(7,9)` would have saved it.

Notably, `pocket-11x11`'s greedy recall under the **baseline** blob config is 0/12 — every single cell fails. Under **reach-more** it improves to 4/12 (roughly 33%). The improvement is entirely from having more frontier tiles to try after the first one locks in — the algorithm didn't change. This is consistent with the trace: with only 4 frontier tiles and a single locked-in L5 path, there's nowhere to escape.

The 9th greedy miss — `3-22.tight-edge-with-chokes` seed 4 at long profile — is the same structural issue in a different guise: individual length-8 and length-6 lanes exist, but greedy's L8 commit boxes out the L6, and there's no cross-lane backtracking to recover.

### Where greedy works

Per the per-board table in §2, plain greedy finds a valid decomposition in:
- **100%** of less-constrained cells (24/24).
- **100%** of cells on `corner-13x13`, `scattered-pockets-13x13`, `tight-corner-1 short`, `sparse-mtns-11x11`, `3.22-fairly-open`.
- **94%** of tight-realistic cells, with the single miss above.
- 33% on `pocket-11x11`, which is the entire story of greedy's weakness.

On realistic blobs, greedy's ordering problems mostly disappear because larger blobs give more frontier tiles and more DFS choice points to escape from a bad first commitment.

### Cheap improvements worth prototyping

- **Face-projection-seeded DFS ordering.** Try frontier tiles in order of max straight-ray depth descending; prefer the deepest direction first in the DFS. This would likely close most `pocket-11x11` cases — face projection correctly identifies `(8,8)`'s non-down directions as promising.
- **Multi-restart.** Try N random DFS seedings per lane, first success wins. Cheap and almost certainly effective.
- **Single-lane backtracking.** If lane i fails, go back one step and try a different path for lane i-1. A modest algorithmic step; probably the right fix if we want to keep greedy principled rather than relying on randomization.

## 5. The blob-pathology finding — baseline vs. reach-more

This is the most important qualitative finding of the session.

### How it surfaced

While inspecting the 16 oracle-fail cells in the baseline run, we noticed all of them lived on exactly two boards at the same two profiles:

- `scattered-pockets-13x13` at medium and long — all 8 cells oracle-infeasible.
- `3.21-real-board-tight-corner-1` at medium and long — all 8 cells oracle-infeasible.

On `scattered-pockets`, the baseline blob (6 tiles, frontier = 2) doesn't escape the horizontal tube. With only 2 frontier tiles adjacent to the blob, `k = 3` is structurally impossible regardless of board geometry — any third lane would have to share a starting tile with another lane.

Bumping the blob config to let random walks punch through the tube walls (5 paths × max length 6) produced 10-tile blobs with frontier = 4–5 (depending on seed), and the oracle instantly flipped to feasible for all 12 cells. **Same board, same general, same lane targets — a larger blob unblocked the decomposition.**

The same pattern held on `tight-corner-1 short` in both configurations, but medium and long profiles on that board did *not* flip with reach-more (see §6).

### Side-by-side overall metrics

| Metric | Baseline | Reach-more | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oracle exists | 68/84 (81%) | 76/84 (90%) | +8 cells |
| Greedy recall | 52/68 (76%) | 67/76 (88%) | +12 pp |
| Face recall | 55/68 (81%) | 58/76 (76%) | −5 pp |
| Face precision | 100% | 100% | — |
| Face false negatives | 13 | 18 | +5 |
| Greedy misses | 16 | 9 | −7 |

### Which cells flipped

- `scattered-pockets-13x13` medium + long: 0/8 → **8/8** oracle-feasible.
- `tight-corner-1` medium + long: 0/8 → 0/8 (unchanged — see §6).
- All other cells: unchanged in oracle status.

Every cell that flipped happened to land in face projection's turning-lanes blind spot (§3), so none of the newly-feasible cells were catchable by face projection. Face false negatives grew from 13 to 18 and face recall as a rate dropped from 81% to 76%. Greedy, by contrast, improved on both axes: recall rose from 76% (52/68) to 88% (67/76). Because blob shapes change between the two runs, we can't decompose greedy's +15 true-positive delta into "new positives caught" vs. "previously-missed cells where reach-more helped" without a per-cell diff, but the aggregate direction is clear.

### Why greedy improves with larger blobs

No algorithmic change. Larger blobs mean more frontier tiles, which means:
1. More choice points after a locked-in first commitment.
2. A higher fraction of frontier tiles with "escape room" for the remaining lanes.
3. Fewer cases where the greedy ordering assumption is fatal.

`pocket-11x11` greedy recall went from 0/12 to 4/12 purely from this effect.

### Two interpretation conclusions

1. **Feasibility is a joint function of (board geometry, blob reach, lane lengths).** The first two co-determine frontier topology, which is the actual load-bearing structure for decomposition. Session 1 argued that "empirical capacity is a joint function of (blob shape, k, lane lengths)" — this session extends the observation: *blob reach* matters as much as blob shape, and it's the dimension our random-walk generator happens to under-produce.

2. **The current random-walk blob generator produces shapes no real solver would enumerate.** Real prefix sets come from an optimizing enumerator that prefers spread-out, high-capture paths. Mulberry32 random walks cluster. On constrained boards they collapse into a small pocket shape that doesn't resemble any prefix a solver would care about. For pre-integration testing, reach-more is a meaningfully better approximation; for real confidence, we need to wire in actual prefix enumerator output.

## 6. What's genuinely hard: `3.21-real-board-tight-corner-1`

Of all 84 cells in the reach-more matrix, the only oracle-fails come from a single board at medium and long profiles: `3.21-real-board-tight-corner-1`. Every other oracle-fail in the baseline run was blob-pathology and dissolved under reach-more.

### Why it survives

The general sits at `(24, 20)` inside a deep corner pocket bounded by the SE board edge, mountains, and more mountains. The reach-more blob (paths = 6, maxLen = 7, seed 1) looks like this:

```
. . . . .
# # . # #
# . . # B
. # . # S
. # . . B
. . B # B
. # B B B
. . # . .
```

8 tiles, frontier = 5. A separate `run-lane-decomp.ts` inspection on this cell reports candidate counts of `L6 = 42`, `L4 = 10`, `L3 = 5` — i.e. plenty of individual lane candidates exist. The blob has grown a bit upward along the pocket perimeter, but the chute that session 1 identified (`"2-3 tile wide chute near (22, 17-21)"`) is still the only real way out.

At the short profile `[5, 3]` the chute can accommodate two non-overlapping lanes because the lanes don't need to fully traverse it:

```
# # . # # .
# . . 1 . .
. # # 1 # #
# # . 1 # B
. . # 1 # S
. 2 # 1 . B
. 2 2 B # B
. . # B B B
. . . # . .
```

(Oracle decomposition: L5 = `[(22,21), (22,20), (22,19), (22,18), (22,17)]`, L3 = `[(21,22), (20,22), (20,21)]`.)

At the medium profile `[6, 4, 3]` the L6 lane has to push further through the chute, and every pair of L6 candidates ends up sharing tiles inside the chute. The enumerator confirms 42 length-6 candidate lanes exist but no 3-tuple of them (with lengths 6, 4, 3) is mutually non-overlapping.

### Profile cliff across configurations

| Profile | Baseline oracle | Reach-more oracle |
|---|---|---|
| short `[5, 3]` | 4/4 | 4/4 |
| medium `[6, 4, 3]` | 0/4 | 0/4 |
| long `[8, 6, 4]` | 0/4 | 0/4 |

The cliff is in the same place under both configurations. Bumping blob reach didn't move it. This is consistent with session 1's length-cliff characterization of this exact board.

### Interpretation

Within the relaxed lane-decomposition formulation (blob as opaque prefix, start anywhere on the frontier, no entry constraints), **`tight-corner-1` has no valid 3-lane decomposition at `L ≥ 6` lengths under any of the 8 random-walk blob instances we tested from its baked-in general position**. The mechanism is structural — the narrow chute that all long lanes must pass through — but the universal claim is about the blob shapes we sampled, not about all possible blob shapes. See the §7 frontier-size-as-feasibility-proxy pointer: a different prefix set that pushes frontier tiles outside the pocket would not necessarily collide the same way.

What this does *not* say:

- It does **not** say `tight-corner-1` is unsolvable by the full solver, and it does **not** say anything about the optimal capture count on this board. The full solver can pick different prefix sets and admissible entry points, and we have not verified what capture count the optimum actually achieves — it may or may not be 24. The session-1 solver does eventually find *a* solution here (it's in `SLOW_SEARCH`, not `IMPOSSIBLE`). The decomposition failure at `L ≥ 6` simply means "this relaxed formulation at these specific lane-length targets has no solution from this general position under our blob shapes," not "no solution exists for the board."
- It does **not** rule out decompositions at these profiles from *different* prefix sets. A prefix set whose tips land outside the pocket mouth would sidestep the chute collision entirely. Integrating with real prefix enumeration (next session) is the right test.

What it *does* say: if the integrated solver hands us a prefix set whose frontier tiles are trapped inside the tight-corner-1 pocket, lane decomposition at `L ≥ 6` is hopeless and the solver should try a different prefix. This is exactly the kind of structural signal a feasibility filter should provide.

## 7. Pointers for future sessions

### Algorithmic

- **Face-projection-seeded greedy.** Use face projection's depth map to order frontier tiles and directions for the greedy DFS. Natural pipeline: face projection's deepest-first assignment also happens to be the best DFS seeding. Expected to close most `pocket-11x11` cases.
- **Tier-3 filter: length-constrained max-flow.** Face projection is one-sided and misses chute collisions that appear at long profiles (the `tight-edge-with-chokes long` cases, the `sparse-mtns-11x11 long` cases, and the `tight-corner-1 medium+long` oracle-fails). A max-flow formulation with per-tile length constraints could catch these while staying cheap. Pairs naturally with face projection as a second filter tier.
- **Single-lane backtracking in greedy.** The pure greedy ceiling is around 88% recall on reach-more blobs. A single step of backtracking (undo lane `i-1` and try again if lane `i` fails) would likely close most of the remaining gap, and is philosophically closer to "good enough" than multi-restart randomization.

### Methodology

- **Realistic prefix blobs.** The largest remaining threat to validity is the blob generator. The random-walk union produces shapes that no prefix enumerator would ever output. Two integration paths:
  1. Hand-pick a few realistic prefix sets per board and feed them directly.
  2. Wire in the prefix enumerator from research-spike-1 and use its output as blob input to the decomposer.
  The second option is deeper but closer to the eventual integration story.
- **Blob generator improvements.** If we keep random-walk blobs around for fast iteration, improvements worth making: diversify walk directions (avoid clustering), optionally constrain to D-depth prefixes only, deduplicate near-identical walks across seeds (session 1 noted seed collisions on `corner-9x9`).
- **Report matrix should include frontier size per cell.** Frontier size is the cheapest feasibility proxy we have (any `k > frontier_size` is structurally infeasible) and we should surface it in the CSV schema so future analyses don't need to re-derive it.

### Scope

- **Admissible entry points, entry overheads.** The brainstorm flagged these as deferred; they're still deferred. Introducing them during heuristic development would obscure which failures are algorithmic vs. constraint-induced. Integration comes after the heuristics are understood in the relaxed formulation.
